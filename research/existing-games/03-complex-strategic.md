# Category 3 — Complex & strategic

The heavyweight end: games with high rule complexity *and* deep strategy — substantial
rules, scoring systems, multiple phases or melds that reward serious study.

## Lead recommendations (best depth-for-complexity)

### 1. Piquet
- **2p fit:** Natively and exclusively two-player; the canonical "serious" two-hander. Outstanding at 2p.
- **Rules:** 32-card pack. Each gets 12 cards; an 8-card talon allows a structured exchange (elder discards up to 5, younger the rest). A declaration phase scores combinations in fixed order: *point* (longest suit), *sequences* (3–8 cards), *sets* (trios/quatorzes of 10+). Then 12 tricks score for leading, capturing, and last trick. A *partie* is six deals; bonuses include *carte blanche*, *repique* (60), *pique* (30), *rubicon*.
- **Complexity vs depth:** Complexity from the rigid declaration sequence and bonus thresholds; depth from the discard decision (balancing point/sequence/set/trick strength under hidden information) plus card counting over only 32 cards, letting experts deduce the opponent's hand.
- **Learning curve:** Hours to play; months to play well.
- **Pedigree:** 16th-century French; centuries of high-level play.
- **Deck:** Stripped 32. No jokers.
- Source: https://www.pagat.com/notrump/piquet.html

### 2. Klaberjass (Clobyosh, Klob, Bela, Kalabriasz)
- **2p fit:** Classic form is native two-player; one of the most skillful two-handers.
- **Rules:** 32-card pack, 9 cards each. Bidding phase to choose trump (accept turn-up, take second suit, or *schmeiss* — offer to abandon). In trump, Jack (*jass*, 20) and 9 (*menel*, 14) outrank Ace/10. Declare *sequences* (twenty/fifty) and *bela* (K+Q trump, 20). Trick play scores card points plus 10 for last trick; bidder must out-score or be *bête*.
- **Complexity vs depth:** Heavy due to inverted trump ranking, sequence declaration etiquette, and the schmeiss mechanic; depth from trump-selection risk assessment and managing the high-value jass/menel.
- **Learning curve:** A few weeks.
- **Pedigree:** Central/Eastern European; spread via Jewish communities; immortalized in Damon Runyon stories.
- **Deck:** Stripped 32. No jokers.
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klaberjass

### 3. Bezique
- **2p fit:** Native two-player melding/trick game; ancestor of Pinochle.
- **Rules:** 64-card double pack. 8 cards each, draw from stock. Trick winner may declare one meld (marriages, *bezique* = Q♠+J♦ for 40, double bezique 500, sequences, four-of-a-kind), then draws. Once stock exhausts, strict follow-suit trick play; *brisques* (aces/tens) score 10 each. Rubicon Bezique scales to four packs.
- **Complexity vs depth:** Complexity from the elaborate meld table and re-melding rules; depth is moderate — timing meld declarations and holding cards to combine is the main skill, but draw luck weighs more than in Piquet/Klaberjass.
- **Learning curve:** Days; the meld schedule is the memorization hurdle.
- **Pedigree:** 19th-century France; Churchill was a devotee; club tournament play.
- **Deck:** Two stripped packs (64). No jokers.
- Source: https://www.pagat.com/marriage/bezique.html

### 4. Two-Handed Pinochle
- **2p fit:** The original Pinochle form; native two-player.
- **Rules:** 48-card pinochle deck (two stripped 9–A packs). 12 cards each, trump turn-up, draw-from-stock phase with melding after winning tricks (runs, marriages, pinochle = J♦+Q♠, "arounds," dix). After stock exhausts, strict follow/win-trick play; capture points. Play to 1000–1200.
- **Complexity vs depth:** Richer meld set than Bezique plus a "must win" endgame; depth from meld-building vs. trick-capture tension.
- **Deck caveat:** Needs a 48-card pinochle deck = two standard decks stripped to 9–A. Not a single standard 52, though buildable from two standard packs.
- **Learning curve:** A week or two.
- **Pedigree:** German-American; large US following.
- Source: https://www.pagat.com/marriage/pin2hand.html

## Strong secondary options

- **Écarté** — Native 2p French casino game; 32-card, 5 each. Elder *proposes* exchanges, younger accepts/refuses, both draw iteratively. King of trump = 1; 3+ tricks = 1; *vole* = 2. Light rules; depth in the propose/refuse meta-game. More medium-complex than heavyweight. https://www.pagat.com/trumps/ecarte.html
- **Honeymoon Bridge** (esp. Semi-Exposed/Great variants) — Family of 2p Bridge adaptations: play throwaway tricks drawing from stock, then auction and play the resulting Bridge hands; "semi-exposed" adds information warfare. Inherits Bridge's enormous complexity but the 2p adaptation dilutes partnership depth and adds draw luck. Best for fluent Bridge players. Standard 52. https://www.pagat.com/auctionwhist/honeymoon.html
- **Two-Handed Canasta** — Recognized 2p variant (draw 2, discard 1; need two canastas to go out). Two 52-card decks + 4 jokers = 108 cards; wild 2s/jokers, red-three bonuses, frozen-pile mechanics, meld thresholds. Rule-heavy; depth at 2p is more tactical (pile control) than the trick games, with high draw luck. https://www.pagat.com/rummy/canasta.html
- **Schnapsen / Sixty-Six** — Native 2p ace-ten point-trick game; 20/24-card deck, trump exchange, two-phase play with "closing the stock," marriages, race to 66. Modest rules but famously deep — sits on the boundary with [category 2](02-simple-but-deep.md). https://www.pagat.com/marriage/schnaps.html

## Player-count corrections

- **Skat** — 3-player only (national game of Germany). Not 2p.
- **Belote** — Primarily 4-player partnership; 2p versions are minor.
- **Gaigel / Binokel** — German-suited 2–4p; require non-standard decks — excluded.

## Synthesis

The best **depth-for-complexity payoff** comes from **Piquet** and **Klaberjass**:
intricate rules where every rule feeds the strategy (Piquet's declaration race and
card counting; Klaberjass's risky trump bidding and inverted ranking). **Schnapsen**
delivers exceptional depth for modest rules — the efficiency champion, if slightly
below the heavyweight rule bar. **Two-Handed Pinochle** and **Bezique** are genuinely
complex and rewarding, but much of their rule-weight is meld-table memorization rather
than decision depth, and the draw adds variance. **Two-Handed Canasta** is the clearest
case of complexity outrunning strategic reward at 2p (fiddly rules, heavy luck,
tactical ceiling). **Honeymoon Bridge** is complex only because it borrows Bridge
wholesale; the 2p adaptation strips the partnership depth that justifies that
complexity. **Écarté** is the lightest here — elegant and strategic, but more
medium-complex than heavyweight.
